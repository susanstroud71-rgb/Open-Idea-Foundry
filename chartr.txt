why is manitoba a patchwork of squares
Manitoba looks like a giant checkerboard from above because the province was literally surveyed and carved into squares—a deliberate, engineered grid imposed on the land during settlement. It’s not natural at all; it’s a human‑made geometric overlay on the Prairies.

Here’s the concise answer first:

Manitoba is a patchwork of squares because of the Dominion Land Survey (DLS), which divided Western Canada into a massive grid of townships and sections for settlement, farming, and land ownership.

Now let’s go deeper, because the real story is fascinating.

🧭 The Dominion Land Survey: Canada’s giant grid machine
Starting in 1871, Canada created one of the largest land‑survey grids on Earth to organize settlement in the Prairies. Manitoba was the first place it was applied.

The DLS divided land into:

Townships — 6 miles × 6 miles

Sections — each township split into 36 squares

Quarter‑sections — each section split again into 4 squares (160 acres each)

This is why Manitoba looks like:

perfect squares

straight roads

rectangular farm fields

uniform rural municipalities

It’s literally a geometric quilt.

🌾 Why squares?
Three big reasons:

1. To rapidly settle the Prairies
Canada wanted to populate the West fast. A grid made it easy to:

assign land to settlers

sell land to railways

plan towns

avoid disputes

A square is simple, repeatable, and easy to map.

2. To support agriculture
Square parcels made farming predictable:

equal‑sized fields

straight roads for hauling grain

easy division among families

It was industrial agriculture before industrial agriculture.

3. To compete with the U.S.
The U.S. had already used a similar grid (the Public Land Survey System).
Canada copied it to:

attract immigrants

avoid losing settlers to the U.S.

create a familiar land system for American farmers moving north

🧊 Why Manitoba looks even more square than Saskatchewan or Alberta
Manitoba was surveyed first, and the grid is:

tighter

more uniform

less interrupted by mountains or badlands

less distorted by curvature corrections

Saskatchewan and Alberta have more “correction lines” where the grid bends slightly. Manitoba is flatter and smaller, so the grid looks cleaner.

🧭 The one exception: southern Manitoba’s weird diagonal lines
If you zoom in near Winnipeg, you’ll see diagonal roads and odd angles.

That’s because the old river‑lot system (Métis and francophone settlement pattern) predates the Dominion Land Survey. River lots were:

long, thin strips

perpendicular to rivers

designed so every family had river access

So Manitoba is actually two systems overlaid:

river lots (older, diagonal, organic)

Dominion grid (newer, square, rigid)

🧨 The result: a province that looks like graph paper from orbit
Manitoba’s “patchwork of squares” is one of the most visible human‑made land patterns on Earth—an artifact of colonization, agriculture, and 19th‑century surveying technology.
